Key Takeaways
  • Hashimoto's is an autoimmune condition where antibodies attack the thyroid. It is the number one cause of hypothyroidism in women.
  • A normal TSH does not rule out Hashimoto's. The TPO and TG antibodies are what show it.
  • Melissa's full thyroid panel is six markers: TSH, free T4, free T3, reverse T3, TPO antibodies and TG antibodies.
  • Free T3 is the active hormone. Reverse T3 acts like the brakes, and a high reverse T3 can cause hypothyroid symptoms even when other levels look fine.
  • Thyroid medication replaces thyroid hormone. It does not treat the autoimmune piece underneath.
  • Postpartum and perimenopause are vulnerable windows, because big hormone shifts affect the immune system.
  • Most of the immune system lives along the gut lining. Melissa tests the gut with a GI-MAP stool test rather than guessing.
  • Oral estrogen can raise the body's need for thyroid hormone by increasing thyroid binding globulin.
  • Her Core Four plate: half non-starchy vegetables, a quarter protein, a quarter complex carbs, and 1 to 2 tablespoons of healthy fat.
  • She adds before she removes. Most women she sees are under eating protein, and low free T3 is associated with too few carbs or calories.
  • Not every woman with Hashimoto's needs to go gluten free. Feeling much better without it often points to a gut problem.
  • Walking in the early days, then weight training two to three days a week once energy starts to come back.
  • The right word is remission, not cure. Antibodies can come down, and results are never guaranteed.
  • The most common mistake: stacking high dose supplements like iodine, selenium or vitamin D without guidance.
Questions Answered

Can you have Hashimoto's with a normal TSH?

Yes. Nurse practitioner Melissa Bunkers had a normal TSH when her own early Hashimoto's was starting. A standard check usually looks at TSH, but thyroid antibodies, TPO and TG, are what show Hashimoto's. If you still have symptoms with a normal TSH, ask your provider whether antibodies and free thyroid hormones were checked.

What is a full thyroid panel?

Melissa Bunkers, NP, uses six markers: TSH, free T4, free T3, reverse T3, TPO antibodies and TG antibodies. Free T3 is the active hormone, reverse T3 acts like the brakes, and the two antibodies show whether Hashimoto's is present. Together they show what thyroid hormones are doing and whether there is an immune piece.

What is Hashimoto's?

Hashimoto's is an autoimmune condition. The body makes proteins called antibodies that attack the thyroid, and over time that damage can leave the thyroid unable to make enough thyroid hormone. Nurse practitioner Melissa Bunkers explains it is an immune system problem first, which is why a hormone number alone does not tell the whole story.

What is the difference between Hashimoto's and hypothyroidism?

Hypothyroidism means the thyroid is not making enough thyroid hormone. Hashimoto's is an immune condition that attacks the thyroid, and Melissa Bunkers, NP, notes it is the number one cause of hypothyroidism in women. Thyroid medication replaces the missing hormone, but it does not address the autoimmune piece underneath.

What does Hashimoto's feel like?

In Melissa Bunkers' experience, common signs are sleeping 8 to 10 hours and waking up exhausted, needing caffeine to get going, body aches, crashing mid afternoon, and feeling wired and tired at night. Brain fog, digestive issues, hives or rashes and food sensitivities can show up too. Talk with your provider if this sounds familiar.

Why is it so hard to lose weight with Hashimoto's?

Melissa Bunkers, NP, sees many women who have under eaten for years, and low free T3 is associated with too few carbohydrates or calories. Cutting calories and adding hard exercise often backfires. She starts with more protein, enough carbohydrates, the Core Four plate, and weight training two to three days a week once energy returns.

Can Hashimoto's be cured?

Melissa Bunkers, NP, does not use the word cure. She says Hashimoto's is not eliminated forever, but it can be put into remission and antibodies can come down. Results vary from person to person and are never guaranteed. Do not stop or change thyroid medication without talking to your prescriber.
