Questions Answered

What is fascia?

Fascia is a three-dimensional web of connective tissue that runs continuously from head to toe. Shauna Fox-Hamilton, MOTR/L, compares it to an orange: the white fibers under the peel, the skins around each wedge and the tiny pockets holding the juice. It supports muscles, bones, nerves and organs, right down to the cells.

What is myofascial release?

Myofascial release is hands-on work that uses gentle, sustained pressure on the fascial system so areas that feel stuck can release. Shauna uses the John F. Barnes approach. She works with her hands only, without lotions or oils, and follows how the tissue responds rather than forcing it.

How is myofascial release different from massage?

Shauna says massage is mostly muscle rubbing, while myofascial release is sustained pressure held long enough to reach deeper layers of fascia. Some massage therapists use myofascial techniques. Her approach goes gentler and longer so the nervous system does not brace, which she says lets the tissue let go.

What does myofascial release feel like?

Shauna says a release can feel like butter melting or a slow taffy stretch. Some people feel warmth, a buzzing or a cooling sensation. Others notice small movements, called unwinding, as the body works out of a stuck pattern. She follows the tissue instead of forcing it.

Is it normal to be sore after myofascial release?

It can be. Shauna says some people feel great and some feel sore for a couple of days, similar to a good workout. Another area may also start talking once one part lets go. She tells clients to drink lots of water, keep moving and listen to their body.

Is fascia blasting safe?

Shauna's concern is intensity. If a tool goes in too hard and too fast, she says the nervous system can feel threatened and brace, which may bring short-term relief without letting deeper tissue release. Her rule: if it hurts so much you cannot let go, ask why you are doing it.

Can you get myofascial release while pregnant?

Yes, according to Shauna. Pregnancy changes alignment and how the pelvis sits, and birth is hard on the body. She says opening up the fascial system can help the body move and function, and she does not promise a specific outcome. After a C-section, she suggests seeing a pelvic floor PT, a myofascial therapist or both.

Shauna Fox-Hamilton, MOTR/L, offers John F. Barnes myofascial release at RoOTed in Sioux Falls, SD. She works with babies, kids, teens and adults, and some chiropractors in the area refer people whose adjustments do not hold. Learn more at therootedot.com.

What is fascia?

Shauna Fox-Hamilton describes fascia as a three-dimensional web of connective tissue that runs continuously from head to toe. It supports everything in the body: muscles, bones, nerves and organs.

She explains it with an orange. Peel it and you see the white fibers connecting the peel to the fruit. That is the first layer. Pull the wedges apart and you find the skins holding each one together. Inside each wedge, tiny pockets hold the juice. That is fascia right down to the cellular level, keeping things moving and gliding.

What is myofascial release?

Shauna uses the John F. Barnes approach to myofascial release. It is a gentle, sustained pressure on the fascial system so spots that are stuck can release.

Fascia can develop what she calls adhesions, which may feel like stiffness or stuckness. In her experience they can come from physical trauma, emotional trauma, surgery, accidents, scar tissue and stress. She works with her hands only, no lotions or oils, and follows the tissue rather than forcing it.

How do you know if your fascia is stuck?

People come in with pain: headaches, backaches, neck, shoulder or knee pain. Others notice they cannot lift an arm as high or get on the floor as easily. Some hold stress in the body, with a tight jaw or shoulders up to their ears.

There is no easy way to see fascia restrictions on imaging yet, she says. An X-ray shows bones and alignment, but not what is pulling you out of alignment.

How is it different from massage, PT and chiropractic care?

Shauna says every one of these has its place. Her approach goes in gentler and holds longer, so the nervous system does not brace and the work can reach deeper layers.

Massage is mostly muscle rubbing, she says. Some massage therapists and PTs do use myofascial techniques. Some chiropractors in Sioux Falls refer people to her when adjustments will not hold, since fascia may be what keeps pulling them out.

What does a session feel like?

The first visit starts with alignment, posture and health history. Most people need a series of sessions, and the goal is maintenance: self-treatment at home and a tune-up when needed.

When fascia lets go, people describe butter melting, a slow taffy stretch, warmth, buzzing or a cooling feeling. Some feel small movements as the body unwinds. Afterward, some feel great and some are sore for a couple of days, like after a good workout.

How do stress and emotions connect to fascia?

When the nervous system is in fight, flight or freeze, Shauna says people brace: a clenched jaw, shoulders up, tight hips. That changes how they move, which can change their fascia.

Fascia is full of nerve endings, and she connects it to proprioception (where your body is in space) and interoception (signals like hunger, a racing heart or butterflies). Some clients have emotional releases on the table. You do not need to be emotionally regulated before you come in.

Who does she work with?

Babies often come for trouble with breastfeeding or bottle feeding, before and after a tongue or lip tie release, or for fussiness. Kids come for nervous system regulation and tension. Teens are often athletes with a first injury.

Adults come for chronic pain, headaches, back, hip, shoulder and jaw pain, and stress held in the body. She also works with women during pregnancy and on scar tissue after surgeries, including C-sections, often alongside pelvic floor PTs.

What do fascia videos on TikTok get right and wrong?

Shauna says creators are right that fascia affects every system, can get tight or stuck, and can affect pain and mobility.

What she wants people to remember: a 30 second video is general information. Someone else's dramatic result may not happen to you. On fascia blasting, her concern is intensity. Too hard and too fast can make the body brace, and it may bring only short-term relief.

Her own example: her plantar fasciitis eased once she treated the front and back of her hip, not only her foot and calf.

How do you find a good myofascial release therapist?

Ask about their background (OT, PT or massage therapist), their specific myofascial training, the tools they use and how they approach the work. Shauna's billboard would read: fascia, it's fascinating. Her sentimental version: listen to your body, it's talking to you.
